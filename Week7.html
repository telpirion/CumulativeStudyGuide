Chapter 10: Statistical Inferences About Two Populations

10.0 Overview & Selecting the Appropriate Test

  • Learning Objectives:

    1. Test hypotheses and develop confidence intervals about the difference in two means with known population variances using the \(z\) statistic.
    2. Test hypotheses and develop confidence intervals about the difference in two means of independent samples with unknown population variances using the \(t\) test.
    3. Test hypotheses and develop confidence intervals about the difference in two dependent (related) populations.
    4. Test hypotheses and develop confidence intervals about the difference in two population proportions.
    5. Test hypotheses about the difference in two population variances using the \(F\) distribution.

  • Transition from Single-Sample to Two-Sample Inference: Chapter 9 focused on one-sample tests: comparing a single sample parameter against an established or hypothesized population benchmark. Chapter 10 extends this inferential framework to structure tests comparing two (2) samples, evaluating whether the observed samples come from the same underlying population or different populations (e.g., Are two brands of toothpaste equally effective? Do two brands of tires wear differently? Does aluminum produced under two different temperatures have the same tensile strength?).

  • Parametric Computations vs. Programmatic R Functions: Demonstration problems presented with summary parameters (sample means, known standard deviations) are evaluated formulaically. When provided with raw observational data, we utilize R’s native statistical testing functions (stats::t.test(), stats::var.test(), and stats::prop.test()).

  • Four Selection Criteria:

    • Parameter Under Study: Difference between two means (\(\mu_1 - \mu_2\)), difference between two proportions (\(p_1 - p_2\)), or ratio of two variances (\(\sigma_1^2 / \sigma_2^2\)).
    • Sampling Structure: Independent random samples vs. related / matched-pairs samples.
    • Variance Availability: Population variances known (\(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2\)) vs. unknown (\(s_1^2, s_2^2\)).
    • Variance Homogeneity: When population variances are unknown, whether equal variances can be assumed (\(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\)) or must be treated as unequal (\(\sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2\)).

10.1 Inferences About the Difference in Two Means: Independent Samples (\(\sigma_1, \sigma_2\) Known)

  • Assumptions:

    • Independent Random Samples: Data drawn randomly and independently from two distinct populations.
    • Known Variances: Both population standard deviations (\(\sigma_1, \sigma_2\)) are established from historical records, accounting audits, or engineering specifications.
    • Normality / Sample Size: Populations are normally distributed or both sample sizes are sufficiently large (\(n_1 \ge 30, n_2 \ge 30\)) under the Central Limit Theorem.

  • Hypothesis Testing:

    • Null Hypothesis: \(H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = D_0\) (frequently \(D_0 = 0\)).
    • Test Statistic (\(z\)):

\[z = \frac{(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) - D_0}{\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}}\]

  • Confidence Interval for \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)\):

\[(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}}\]

  • Slide Examples from Ken Black (9th Edition):
    • Example 1: Hypothesis Test on Annual Manager Wages: Comparing annual salaries of advertising managers (Population 1: \(n_1 = 32\), \(\bar{x}_1 = \$70.700\text{k}\), known \(\sigma_1 = 16.253\)) versus auditing managers (Population 2: \(n_2 = 34\), \(\bar{x}_2 = \$62.187\text{k}\), known \(\sigma_2 = 12.900\)) at \(\alpha = 0.05\) (\(z_{\text{crit}} = \pm 1.96\)). \[z = \frac{(70.700 - 62.187) - 0}{\sqrt{\frac{16.253^2}{32} + \frac{12.900^2}{34}}} = \frac{8.513}{3.626} = 2.35 > 1.96 \implies \text{Reject } H_0 \; (p = 0.0189)\] Corresponding 95% Confidence Interval: \(8.513 \pm 1.96(3.626) = [1.41, 15.63]\) thousand dollars. Because 0 is not in the interval, \(H_0\) is rejected.
    • Example 2: Confidence Interval on Grocery Coupon Savings: Estimating weekly coupon savings for middle-income shoppers (\(n_1 = 60, \bar{x}_1 = \$5.84, \sigma_1 = \$1.41\)) vs. low-income shoppers (\(n_2 = 80, \bar{x}_2 = \$2.67, \sigma_2 = \$0.54\)) at 98% confidence (\(z_{0.01} = 2.33\)): \[(5.84 - 2.67) \pm 2.33 \sqrt{\frac{1.41^2}{60} + \frac{0.54^2}{80}} = 3.17 \pm 2.33(0.1918) = [\$2.72, \$3.62]\]
    • Software Note: While Excel includes this procedure in its Data Analysis Toolpak (z-Test: Two Sample for Means), Minitab does not have a native two-sample \(z\)-test command.

10.2 Inferences About the Difference in Two Means: Independent Samples (\(\sigma_1, \sigma_2\) Unknown)

When population standard deviations are unknown, sample standard deviations (\(s_1, s_2\)) are used, requiring the Student’s \(t\)-distribution.

Case A: Equal Variances Assumed (\(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\)) — Pooled-Variance \(t\)-Test

  • Pooled Sample Variance (\(s_p^2\)): Weighted average of individual sample variances:

\[s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}\]

  • Test Statistic (\(t\)):

\[t = \frac{(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) - D_0}{\sqrt{s_p^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}} \quad \text{with } df = n_1 + n_2 - 2\]

  • Confidence Interval:

\[(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t_{\alpha/2, df} \sqrt{s_p^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}\]

Case B: Unequal Variances (\(\sigma_1^2 \ne \sigma_2^2\)) — Welch’s Satterthwaite \(t\)-Test

  • Test Statistic (\(t\)):

\[t = \frac{(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) - D_0}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}\]

  • Degrees of Freedom (\(df\)): Computed using the Welch–Satterthwaite approximation:

\[df = \frac{\left(\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}\right)^2}{\frac{(s_1^2 / n_1)^2}{n_1 - 1} + \frac{(s_2^2 / n_2)^2}{n_2 - 1}}\]

  • Slide Examples from Ken Black (9th Edition):
    • Example 1: Hernandez Manufacturing Company Training Program (Table 10.2): Management tests knowledge scores for new employees assigned to traditional 3-day lecture training (Method A: \(n_1 = 15\)) vs. an experimental 2-day DVD program (Method B: \(n_2 = 12\)) at \(\alpha = 0.05\) (\(df = 25, t_{\text{crit}} = \pm 2.060\)). \[\bar{x}_1 = 47.73, \; s_1^2 = 19.495 \quad \text{vs.} \quad \bar{x}_2 = 56.50, \; s_2^2 = 18.273\] \[s_p^2 = \frac{14(19.495) + 11(18.273)}{25} = 18.957 \implies t = \frac{47.73 - 56.50}{\sqrt{18.957(1/15 + 1/12)}} = -5.20\] Because \(-5.20 < -2.060\), reject \(H_0\). Method B scores are \(>8\) points higher, justifying adoption of the shorter training curriculum.
    • Example 2: Conscientiousness Personality Study (Table 10.3): Comparing conscientiousness between phone survey respondents (\(n_1 = 9, \bar{x}_1 = 37.09, s_1 = 1.727\)) and average Americans (\(n_2 = 10, \bar{x}_2 = 34.99, s_2 = 1.253\)). With \(df = 17\) and \(t_{0.005, 17} = 2.898\), the 99% CI is \(2.10 \pm 2.898(0.6868) = [0.11, 4.09]\).

10.3 Inferences for Two Related Populations (Matched-Pairs \(t\)-Test)

  • Context & Requirements: Matched pairs, pre-test/post-test designs, repeated measures, or naturally paired subjects (e.g., twins, siblings, spouses). Samples must be dependent, and sample sizes must be strictly identical (\(n_1 = n_2 = n\)).

  • Concept: Eliminates extraneous subject-to-subject variability by converting paired observations into a single sample of differences:

\[d_i = x_{1i} - x_{2i}\]

  • Sample Metrics of Differences:

    • Mean Difference (\(\bar{d}\)): \(\bar{d} = \frac{\sum d_i}{n}\)
    • Standard Deviation of Differences (\(s_d\)): \(s_d = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (d_i - \bar{d})^2}{n - 1}}\)

  • Hypothesis Testing:

    • Null Hypothesis: \(H_0: \mu_d = D_0\) (frequently directional: \(H_0: \mu_d \le 0\) or two-tailed \(H_0: \mu_d = 0\)).
    • Test Statistic (\(t\)):

\[t = \frac{\bar{d} - D_0}{\frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}}} \quad \text{with } df = n - 1\]

  • Confidence Interval for Paired Differences:

\[\bar{d} \pm t_{\alpha/2, n-1} \left(\frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\]

  • Slide Examples from Ken Black (9th Edition):
    • Example 1: Common Stock P/E Ratios Across Two Years (Tables 10.5 & 10.6): A researcher samples \(n = 9\) firms from the Handbook of Common Stocks to test whether P/E ratios changed significantly between Year 1 and Year 2 at \(\alpha = 0.01\) (\(df = 8, t_{\text{crit}} = \pm 3.355\)). \[\bar{d} = -5.033, \quad s_d = 21.599 \implies t = \frac{-5.033 - 0}{21.599 / \sqrt{9}} = -0.70\] Since \(-3.355 < -0.70 < 3.355\), fail to reject \(H_0\) (\(p = 0.504\)). There is insufficient evidence of a shift in P/E ratios.
    • Example 2: Indianapolis New-House Sales Confidence Interval: Estimating the annual difference in new-house sales per firm across 18 real estate agencies between May 2015 and May 2016 (\(n = 18, \bar{d} = -3.389, s_d = 3.274\)). With \(df = 17\) and \(t_{0.005, 17} = 2.898\), the 99% CI is \(-3.389 \pm 2.898(0.7717) = [-5.625, -1.153]\) houses per firm.
    • Demonstration Problem 10.5 (p. 341): Testing consumer ratings before and after a video campaign (\(n = 7, \alpha = 0.05\), directional test alternative = "greater").

10.4 Statistical Inferences About Two Population Proportions (\(p_1 - p_2\))

  • Assumptions: Independent random samples with adequate sample sizes: \(n_1 p_1 \ge 5\), \(n_1(1-p_1) \ge 5\), \(n_2 p_2 \ge 5\), and \(n_2(1-p_2) \ge 5\).

  • Hypothesis Testing:

    • Under \(H_0: p_1 - p_2 = 0\), both groups share a common population proportion (\(p\)). Compute the pooled sample proportion (\(\bar{p}\)):

\[\bar{p} = \frac{x_1 + x_2}{n_1 + n_2} = \frac{n_1 \hat{p}_1 + n_2 \hat{p}_2}{n_1 + n_2}, \quad \bar{q} = 1 - \bar{p}\]

  • Test Statistic (\(z\)):

\[z = \frac{(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) - 0}{\sqrt{\bar{p}\bar{q}\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}}\]

  • Confidence Interval for \((p_1 - p_2)\):

    • Uses unpooled individual sample proportions:

\[(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2) \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}_1(1 - \hat{p}_1)}{n_1} + \frac{\hat{p}_2(1 - \hat{p}_2)}{n_2}}\]

  • Slide Examples from Ken Black (9th Edition):
    • Example 1: Consumer vs. CEO Ethical Perceptions: Testing whether a significantly higher proportion of consumers than CEOs believe fear of getting caught or losing one’s job strongly influences ethical behavior. Consumers (\(n_1 = 755, \hat{p}_1 = 0.57\)) vs. CEOs (\(n_2 = 616, \hat{p}_2 = 0.50\)) at \(\alpha = 0.10\) (\(z_{\text{crit}} = 1.28\)). \[\bar{p} = \frac{755(0.57) + 616(0.50)}{755 + 616} = 0.539 \implies z = \frac{0.57 - 0.50}{\sqrt{(0.539)(0.461)(1/755 + 1/616)}} = 2.59\] Because \(z = 2.59 > 1.28\) (\(p = 0.0048 < 0.10\)), reject \(H_0\). Consumers are significantly more likely to cite fear as an ethical driver.
    • Example 2: Supermarket Shopper Demographics: Estimating the difference in the proportion of male shoppers between morning (\(n_1 = 400, x_1 = 48 \implies \hat{p}_1 = 0.12\)) and after-5 p.m. (\(n_2 = 480, x_2 = 187 \implies \hat{p}_2 \approx 0.39\)) at 98% confidence (\(z_{0.01} = 2.33\)): \[(0.12 - 0.39) \pm 2.33 \sqrt{\frac{0.12(0.88)}{400} + \frac{0.39(0.61)}{480}} = -0.27 \pm 0.064 = [-0.334, -0.206]\]

10.5 Testing Hypotheses About Two Population Variances (\(F\)-Test)

  • Purpose & Applications: Evaluates whether two populations have equal variance (\(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\)), commonly used to evaluate relative risk in finance, to compare process consistency in manufacturing, or to test equal variance assumptions prior to pooling \(t\)-tests.

  • Distribution & Assumptions:

    • \(F\)-Distribution: Defined by numerator degrees of freedom (\(df_1 = \nu_1 = n_1 - 1\)) and denominator degrees of freedom (\(df_2 = \nu_2 = n_2 - 1\)). Strictly positive and right-skewed.
    • Strict Normality: Highly sensitive to departures from normality; populations must be normally distributed.

  • Test Statistic (\(F\)):

\[F = \frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2}\]

  • Slide Examples from Ken Black (9th Edition):
    • Example 1: Metal Sheet Thickness Consistency (Two Machines): Machine 1 (\(n_1 = 10, s_1^2 = 0.11378\)) and Machine 2 (\(n_2 = 12, s_2^2 = 0.02023\)) are compared for 22 mm sheet thickness consistency at \(\alpha = 0.05\) (\(df_1 = 9, df_2 = 11\)). Upper critical value: \(F_{0.025, 9, 11} = 3.59\). Lower critical value: \(F_{0.975, 9, 11} \approx 0.28\). \[F = \frac{0.11378}{0.02023} = 5.62 > 3.59 \implies \text{Reject } H_0\] Machine 1 produces significantly greater variability; operators should investigate Machine 1 for root cause maintenance.

Ethical Considerations in Comparative Inferential Analysis

  • Validating Distributional Assumptions: Verifying that data satisfy underlying distributional requirements (especially strict normality for the \(F\)-test and adequate sample sizes for proportion approximations) before interpreting test statistics.
  • Pre-Specifying Hypotheses: Defining the null and alternative hypotheses, directional status (one-tailed vs. two-tailed), and significance level (\(\alpha\)) prior to data inspection to prevent “p-hacking” or post-hoc justification.
  • Reporting Effect Sizes & Confidence Intervals: Presenting confidence intervals alongside \(p\)-values to communicate the practical magnitude and uncertainty of observed differences, avoiding over-reliance on bare statistical significance.
  • Preserving Study Design Integrity: Accurately distinguishing between independent and paired designs. Treating paired data as independent artificially alters the variance estimate and degrees of freedom, undermining inferential validity.

Inferences About Two Means: Independent Samples (\(\sigma\) Known) — Questions 1–12

1. A researcher wants to estimate the difference in the means of two populations. A random sample of 40 items from the first population results in a sample mean of 433 with a population standard deviation of 112. A random sample of 50 items from the second population results in a sample mean of 467 with a population standard deviation of 120. From this information, a point estimate of the difference of population means can be computed as _______.

  • Answer: -34
  • Solution: \[\text{Point Estimate} = \bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2 = 433 - 467 = -34\]

2. Using the information from question 1, the researcher can compute a 95% confidence interval to estimate the difference in population means. The resulting confidence interval is _______.

  • Answer: -82.07 <= µ1 - µ2 <= 14.07 (or [-82.07, 14.07])
  • Solution: \[\sigma_{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{112^2}{40} + \frac{120^2}{50}} = \sqrt{313.6 + 288} = \sqrt{601.6} \approx 24.5275\] For 95% confidence, \(z_{\alpha/2} = 1.96\): \[(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm z \cdot \sigma_{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2} = -34 \pm 1.96(24.5275) = -34 \pm 48.074 \implies [-82.07, 14.07]\]

3. A random sample of 32 items is taken from a population which has a population variance of 93. The resulting sample mean is 45.6. A random sample of 37 items is taken from a population which has a population variance of 88. The resulting sample mean is 49.4. Using this information, a 98% confidence interval can be computed to estimate the difference in means of these two populations. The resulting interval is _______.

  • Answer: -9.16 <= µ1 - µ2 <= 1.56 (or [-9.16, 1.56])
  • Solution: \[\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2 = 45.6 - 49.4 = -3.8\] \[\sigma_{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2} = \sqrt{\frac{93}{32} + \frac{88}{37}} = \sqrt{2.90625 + 2.37838} = \sqrt{5.28463} \approx 2.2988\] For 98% confidence (\(\alpha = 0.02 \implies \alpha/2 = 0.01\)), \(z_{0.01} = 2.33\): \[-3.8 \pm 2.33(2.2988) = -3.8 \pm 5.356 \implies [-9.16, 1.56]\]

4. A researcher desires to estimate the difference in means of two populations. To accomplish this, he/she takes a random sample of 85 items from the first population. The sample yields a mean of 168 with a population variance of 783. A random sample of 70 items is taken from the second population yielding a mean of 161 with a population variance of 780. A 94% confidence interval is computed to estimate the difference in population means. The resulting confidence interval is _______.

  • Answer: -1.48 <= µ1 - µ2 <= 15.48 (or [-1.48, 15.48])
  • Solution: \[\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2 = 168 - 161 = 7\] \[\sigma_{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2} = \sqrt{\frac{783}{85} + \frac{780}{70}} = \sqrt{9.21176 + 11.14286} = \sqrt{20.3546} \approx 4.5116\] For 94% confidence (\(\alpha = 0.06 \implies \alpha/2 = 0.03\)), \(z_{0.03} = 1.88\): \[7 \pm 1.88(4.5116) = 7 \pm 8.4818 \implies [-1.48, 15.48]\]

5. Is there a difference in the average number years of experience of assembly line employees between company A and company B? A researcher wants to conduct a statistical test to answer this question. He is likely to be conducting a _______-tailed test.

  • Answer: Two
  • Solution: Because the research question asks whether “there is a difference” without asserting directionality, the hypotheses are \(H_0: \mu_A - \mu_B = 0\) versus \(H_a: \mu_A - \mu_B \ne 0\), necessitating a two-tailed test.

6. The researcher who is conducting the test to determine if there is a difference in the average number of years of experience of assembly line workers between companies A and B is using an alpha of .10. The critical value of z for this problem is _______.

  • Answer: ± 1.645 (or 1.645)
  • Solution: For a two-tailed test with \(\alpha = 0.10\), the rejection area is split equally into \(\alpha/2 = 0.05\) per tail. The critical values are \(z = \pm 1.645\).

7. Suppose the researcher conducting an experiment to compare the ages of workers at two companies. The researcher randomly samples forty-five assembly-line workers from company A and discovers that the sample average is 7.1 years with a population standard deviation of 2.3. Fifty-two assembly-line workers from company B are randomly selected resulting in a sample average of 6.2 years and a population standard deviation of 2.7. The observed z value for this problem is _______.

  • Answer: 1.77
  • Solution: \[z = \frac{(\bar{x}_A - \bar{x}_B) - 0}{\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_A^2}{n_A} + \frac{\sigma_B^2}{n_B}}} = \frac{7.1 - 6.2}{\sqrt{\frac{2.3^2}{45} + \frac{2.7^2}{52}}} = \frac{0.9}{\sqrt{0.11756 + 0.14019}} = \frac{0.9}{\sqrt{0.25775}} = \frac{0.9}{0.50769} \approx 1.77\]

8. Using an alpha of .10 and the critical values determined in questions 6 and 7, the decision is to _______ the null hypothesis.

  • Answer: Reject
  • Solution: Because observed \(z = 1.77 > z_{\text{crit}} = 1.645\), the test statistic falls in the upper rejection region. The decision is to reject the null hypothesis.

9. A researcher has a theory that the mean for population A is less than the mean for population B. To test this, she randomly samples thirty-eight items from population A and determines that the sample average is 38.4 with a population variance of 50.5 She randomly samples thirty-two items from population B and determines that the sample average is 44.3 with a population variance of 48.6 Alpha is .05. She is going to conduct a _______-tailed test.

  • Answer: One (specifically, left-tailed)
  • Solution: The alternative hypothesis specifies a strict direction (\(H_a: \mu_A < \mu_B \iff \mu_A - \mu_B < 0\)), making this a one-tailed (left-tailed) test.

10. Using the information from question 9, the critical z value is _______.

  • Answer: -1.645
  • Solution: For a left-tailed test with \(\alpha = 0.05\), the entire rejection region of area 0.05 is placed in the lower tail: \(z_{\text{crit}} = -1.645\).

11. Using the information from question 9, the observed value of z is _______.

  • Answer: -3.50
  • Solution: \[z = \frac{38.4 - 44.3}{\sqrt{\frac{50.5}{38} + \frac{48.6}{32}}} = \frac{-5.9}{\sqrt{1.32895 + 1.51875}} = \frac{-5.9}{\sqrt{2.8477}} = \frac{-5.9}{1.6875} \approx -3.50\]

12. Using the results determined in question 10 and 11, the decision is to _______ the null hypothesis.

  • Answer: Reject

Inferences About Two Means: Independent Samples (\(\sigma\) Unknown) — Questions 13–19

13. A researcher is interested in testing to determine if the mean of population one is greater than the mean of population two. He uses the following hypotheses to test this theory: \[H_0: \mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0\] \[H_a: \mu_1 - \mu_2 > 0\] He randomly selects a sample of 8 items from population one resulting in a mean of 14.7 and a standard deviation of 3.4. He randomly selects a sample of 12 items from population two resulting in a mean of 11.5 and a standard deviation 2.9. He is using an alpha value of .10 to conduct this test. The degrees of freedom for this problem are _______. It is assumed that these values are normally distributed in both populations.

  • Answer: 18
  • Solution: For pooled-variance two-sample \(t\)-tests with assumed equal variances: \[df = n_1 + n_2 - 2 = 8 + 12 - 2 = 18\]

14. The critical table t value used to conduct the hypothesis test in question 13 is _______.

  • Answer: 1.33 (or 1.330)
  • Solution: For a right-tailed test (\(H_a: \mu_1 - \mu_2 > 0\)) with \(\alpha = 0.10\) and \(df = 18\), the critical value is \(t_{0.10, 18} = 1.330\).

15. The observed t value from the sample data is ______.

  • Answer: 2.26
  • Solution: \[s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2 + (n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2} = \frac{7(3.4)^2 + 11(2.9)^2}{18} = \frac{7(11.56) + 11(8.41)}{18} = \frac{173.43}{18} \approx 9.635\] \[t = \frac{(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) - 0}{\sqrt{s_p^2 \left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}} = \frac{14.7 - 11.5}{\sqrt{9.635 \left(\frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{12}\right)}} = \frac{3.2}{\sqrt{9.635(0.20833)}} = \frac{3.2}{\sqrt{2.00729}} = \frac{3.2}{1.4168} \approx 2.26\]

16. Based on the observed t value obtained in question 15 and the critical table t value in question 14, the researcher should _______ the null hypothesis.

  • Answer: Reject
  • Solution: Because observed \(t = 2.26 > t_{\text{crit}} = 1.33\), the test statistic falls in the upper rejection region (\(p = 0.018\)). The researcher should reject the null hypothesis.

17. What is the difference in the means of two populations? A researcher wishes to determine this by taking random samples of size 14 from each population and computing a 90% confidence interval. The point estimate for the difference in the means of these two populations is _______. Assume that the values are normally distributed in each population.

  • Answer: -110
  • Solution: \(\text{Point Estimate} = \bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2 = 780 - 890 = -110\).

18. The table t value used to construct the confidence interval for the problem in question 17 is _______.

  • Answer: 1.706
  • Solution: Degrees of freedom \(df = n_1 + n_2 - 2 = 14 + 14 - 2 = 26\). For a 90% confidence interval (\(\alpha/2 = 0.05\)), the critical value is \(t_{0.05, 26} = 1.706\).

19. The confidence interval constructed for the problem in question 17 is _______.

  • Answer: -271.56 <= µ1 - µ2 <= 51.56 (or [-271.56, 51.56])
  • Solution: \[s_p^2 = \frac{13(245^2) + 13(256^2)}{26} = \frac{60025 + 65536}{2} = 62780.5\] \[\text{SE} = \sqrt{s_p^2 \left(\frac{1}{14} + \frac{1}{14}\right)} = \sqrt{\frac{62780.5}{7}} = \sqrt{8968.64} \approx 94.703\] \[-110 \pm 1.706(94.703) = -110 \pm 161.56 \implies [-271.56, 51.56]\]

20. The matched-pairs t test deals with _______ samples.

  • Solution: Matched-pairs designs evaluate paired or repeated measurements obtained from the same or matched subjects, representing related (dependent) samples.

  • Solution: Because observed \(t = -10.17 < t_{\text{crit}} = -2.681\), the test statistic falls deeply into the lower rejection region. The decision is to reject the null hypothesis.

Inferences About Two Population Proportions — Questions 34–41

34. A researcher is interested in estimating the difference in two populations proportions. A sample of 1000 from each population results in sample proportions of .61 and .64. The point estimate of the difference in the population proportions is _______.

  • Answer: -.03 (or -0.03)
  • Solution: \(\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 = 0.61 - 0.64 = -0.03\).

35. Using the data from question 34, the researcher computes a 90% confidence interval to estimate the difference in population proportions. The resulting confidence interval is _______.

  • Answer: -.066 <= p1 - p2 <= .006 (or [-0.066, 0.006])
  • Solution: \[\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{p_1(1 - p_1)}{n_1} + \frac{p_2(1 - p_2)}{n_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.61(0.39)}{1000} + \frac{0.64(0.36)}{1000}} = \sqrt{0.0004683} \approx 0.02164\] For 90% confidence, \(z_{\alpha/2} = 1.645\): \[-0.03 \pm 1.645(0.02164) = -0.03 \pm 0.0356 \implies [-0.066, 0.006]\]

36. A random sample of 400 items from a population shows that 110 of the sample items possess a given characteristic. A random sample of 550 items from a second population resulted in 154 of the sample items possessing the characteristic. Using this data, a 99% confidence interval is constructed to estimate the difference in population proportions which possess the given characteristic. The resulting confidence interval is _______.

  • Answer: -.081 <= p1 - p2 <= .071 (or [-0.081, 0.071])
  • Solution: \[\hat{p}_1 = \frac{110}{400} = 0.275, \quad \hat{p}_2 = \frac{154}{550} = 0.280 \implies \hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 = -0.005\] \[\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.275(0.725)}{400} + \frac{0.280(0.720)}{550}} = \sqrt{0.0004984 + 0.0003665} = \sqrt{0.000865} \approx 0.02941\] For 99% confidence, \(z_{0.005} = 2.575\) (or \(2.58\)): \[-0.005 \pm 2.575(0.02941) = -0.005 \pm 0.0757 \implies [-0.081, 0.071]\]

37. A researcher desires to estimate the difference in proportions of two populations. To accomplish this, he/she samples 338 and 332 items respectively from each population. The resulting sample proportions are .71 and .68 respectively. Using this data, a 90% confidence interval can be computed to estimate the difference in population proportions. The resulting confidence interval is _______.

  • Answer: -.0285 <= p1 - p2 <= .0885 (or [-0.0285, 0.0885])
  • Solution: \[\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2 = 0.71 - 0.68 = 0.03\] \[\text{SE} = \sqrt{\frac{0.71(0.29)}{338} + \frac{0.68(0.32)}{332}} = \sqrt{0.0006092 + 0.0006554} = \sqrt{0.0012646} \approx 0.03556\] For 90% confidence, \(z = 1.645\): \[0.03 \pm 1.645(0.03556) = 0.03 \pm 0.0585 \implies [-0.0285, 0.0885]\]

38. A statistician is being asked to test a new theory that the proportion of population A possessing a given characteristic is greater than the proportion of population B possessing the characteristic. A random sample of 625 from population A has been taken and it is determined that 463 possess the characteristic. A random sample of 704 taken from population B results in 428 possessing the characteristic. The alternative hypothesis for this problem is _______.

  • Answer: pA – pB > 0 (or \(H_a: p_A > p_B\))
  • Solution: Testing whether proportion A is greater than proportion B translates to the directional alternative hypothesis \(H_a: p_A - p_B > 0\).

39. The observed value of z for question 38 is _______.

  • Answer: 5.14
  • Solution: \[\hat{p}_A = \frac{463}{625} = 0.7408, \quad \hat{p}_B = \frac{428}{704} \approx 0.6080\] \[\bar{p} = \frac{463 + 428}{625 + 704} = \frac{891}{1329} \approx 0.67043, \quad \bar{q} = 1 - \bar{p} \approx 0.32957\] \[\text{SE}_{\text{pool}} = \sqrt{\bar{p}\bar{q}\left(\frac{1}{n_A} + \frac{1}{n_B}\right)} = \sqrt{(0.22096)\left(\frac{1}{625} + \frac{1}{704}\right)} = \sqrt{0.0006673} \approx 0.02583\] \[z = \frac{\hat{p}_A - \hat{p}_B}{\text{SE}_{\text{pool}}} = \frac{0.7408 - 0.6080}{0.02583} = \frac{0.1328}{0.02583} \approx 5.14\]

40. Suppose alpha is .10. The critical value of z for question 38 is _______.

  • Answer: 1.28
  • Solution: For a right-tailed test (\(H_a: p_A - p_B > 0\)) with \(\alpha = 0.10\), the critical value is \(z_{0.10} = 1.28\) (or \(1.282\)).

41. Based on the results of question 39 and 40, the decision for the problem in question 38 is to _______ the null hypothesis.

  • Answer: Reject
  • Solution: Because observed \(z = 5.14 > z_{\text{crit}} = 1.28\), the test statistic falls far into the upper rejection region. The decision is to reject the null hypothesis.

Testing Hypotheses About Two Population Variances — Questions 42–44

42. In testing hypotheses about two population variances, use the _______ distribution.

  • Answer: F
  • Solution: The sampling distribution of the ratio of two independent sample variances from normally distributed populations is the \(F\)-distribution.

43. Suppose we want to test the following hypothesis: \[H_0: \sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 \quad \text{and} \quad H_a: \sigma_1^2 > \sigma_2^2\] A sample of 9 items from population one yielded a sample standard deviation of 8.6. A sample of 8 items from population two yielded a sample standard deviation of 6.9. If alpha is .05, the critical F value is _______.

  • Answer: 3.73
  • Solution: Numerator degrees of freedom: \(df_1 = n_1 - 1 = 9 - 1 = 8\). Denominator degrees of freedom: \(df_2 = n_2 - 1 = 8 - 1 = 7\). For a right-tailed test with \(\alpha = 0.05\), \(F_{0.05, 8, 7} = 3.73\).

44. The observed F value for question 43 (referenced as question 45 in original exam prompt) is _______. The resulting decision is _______.

  • Answer: 1.55, Fail to Reject the Null Hypothesis
  • Solution: \[F = \frac{s_1^2}{s_2^2} = \frac{8.6^2}{6.9^2} = \frac{73.96}{47.61} \approx 1.55\] Because observed \(F = 1.55 < F_{\text{crit}} = 3.73\), the sample variance ratio does not exceed the critical value. The resulting decision is to fail to reject the null hypothesis.
